Observatory

Light and matter near a black hole

Bend light with the lens, heat the orbiting ensemble with noise, and launch a pulse to watch a wavefront wrap around the photon sphere.

Instrument noteLight follows exact null geodesics of the Schwarzschild metric in the equatorial plane, u″ + u = (3/2) rs u², drawn on Flamm's paraboloid, with each wavefront at a single Schwarzschild coordinate time. Matter follows timelike geodesics in proper time, coupled to a Langevin bath with friction and noise of strength σ. Click or tap the sheet to emit a spherical pulse.

Behind the titleThe accretion disk at the top of the page is a simulation too: test particles in the Paczyński–Wiita potential, which places the innermost stable orbit at 6GM/c², seen 8° above the disk plane, with light from its far side bent up over the hole.

Research

Two questions run through my work. When we describe a system with fewer variables than it really has, what do we lose, and can we bound it? And when a model learns from noisy data, which features of what it learned are stable, and which are artifacts of the noise? The tools are shared: path-space relative entropy, spectral theory, and the language of phase transitions.

Quantum thermodynamicsWorking paper

Hidden entropy production under coarse-graining

Entropy production measures how distinguishable a process is from its time reverse. An observer who sees only some of the variables always measures less of it. I study how much less, in an exactly solvable Gaussian model of an open quantum system.

σobs ≤ σtot Data processing for path-space relative entropy: coarse-graining can only lose irreversibility. The question is how much.

In this model the phase-space (Wigner) description underestimates irreversibility by a computable, purely quantum factor that diverges as the temperature goes to zero. Part of the story is absolute irreversibility: forward trajectories whose time reverses are impossible.

Machine learning theoryIn revision

Spectral response of normalizing flows trained on noisy data

A normalizing flow learns an invertible map, so its Jacobian is available everywhere. I use the spectrum of the learned inverse map's Jacobian as a diagnostic. A Stieltjes response order parameter S(ε) and its susceptibility χS(ε) track how that spectrum reorganizes as the training noise ε grows, and mark the range of noise levels over which the learned model is robust.

S(z) = ∫ ρ(λ) dλ ⁄ (λ − z) The Stieltjes transform of a spectral density ρ, the object the response order parameter is built from.

Experiments use RealNVP models on natural images (BSD68), speech (LibriSpeech) and CIFAR, swept across a 60-point grid of training-noise levels on Boston University's Shared Computing Cluster.

Learning theoryIn progress

When does training noise help?

Adding Gaussian noise during training often improves predictions. That is a claim that needs a proof, not a rule of thumb. I model the problem with two noise sources, the noise already in the observed data and the noise injected in training, and ask which injected level yields the model that best fits the underlying distribution. The answer organizes into three regimes.

Current work applies this picture to Fourier-domain analyses of model robustness, with validation planned on CIFAR-10.

About

I'm a researcher and educator at Boston University, working where statistical physics meets machine learning theory.

My interests run across pure mathematics, theoretical physics and machine learning. I'm happiest when a heuristic can be made precise enough to prove or refute, and I tend to reach for the tools of statistical mechanics to do it: relative entropy, response functions, and phase transitions.

Statistical mechanics and condensed matter are long-standing loves. Landau theory, the Ising model and the renormalization group show up in my research, in my notes, and in the figures I draw for them.

I teach MA 214, Applied Statistics, and I'm writing several textbooks, including one on statistical field theory and one on graduate mathematical statistics.
