Observatory

Light and matter near a black hole

Bend light with the lens, heat the orbiting ensemble with noise, and launch a pulse to watch a wavefront wrap around the photon sphere.

Instrument noteLight follows exact null geodesics of the Schwarzschild metric in the equatorial plane, u″ + u = (3/2) rs u², drawn on Flamm's paraboloid, with each wavefront at a single Schwarzschild coordinate time. Matter follows timelike geodesics in proper time, coupled to a Langevin bath with friction and noise of strength σ. Click or tap the sheet to emit a spherical pulse.

Behind the titleThe accretion disk at the top of the page is a simulation too: test particles in the Paczyński–Wiita potential, which places the innermost stable orbit at 6GM/c², seen 8° above the disk plane, with light from its far side bent up over the hole.

Binary

Two black holes in orbit

Bring the pair closer or make them heavier: the orbit quickens, and the gravitational waves it sends out grow shorter and stronger.

Instrument noteThe holes follow a circular Kepler orbit, Ω² = GM/a³, and the sheet sags into their combined Newtonian potential. The ripples are the leading-order (quadrupole) wave in the orbital plane, h ∝ (μM/ar) cos 2[Φ(t − r/c) − φ]: two spiral arms at twice the orbital frequency, fading as 1/r. Each ripple carries the orbit as it was when it left, so a change spreads outward at the wave speed. The ripples are drawn about 20 times slower than light and taller than life. The trace is the strain seen from above the orbit; its times and frequencies are real for the masses shown.

Research

I work on physics and machine learning, and the surprisingly rich interplay between them.

On the physics side, I am specifically interested in stochastic thermodynamics, which is a framework that extends statistical mechanics to systems that are out-of-equilibrium. The scale of applications can range from the very very small (in open quantum systems) to the very very large (stochastic inflation in cosmology).

The machine learning community in recent years has concentrated its center of mass to studying LLMs and other generative models. This probably means that I have migrated to somewhere in that neighborhood as well. I am interested in studying how far out-of-distribution (OOD) machine learning models can generalize.

Quantum Thermodynamics

The Arrow Of Time

Quantum thermodynamics, as the name suggests, is about combining quantum mechanics with thermodynamics. The difference between this and quantum statistical mechanics is the former's ability to describe the dynamics of systems that are not in thermal equilibrium. One of the most interesting (at least, for me) consequences of quantum thermo is the microscopic description for the arrow of time. To put it short, we can distinguish the arrow of time by measuring the amount of entropy that is produced when a system undergoes some stochastic evolution. My work in this project showed that coarse grained measurements underestimate irreversibility by a purely quantum factor that diverges as the temperature goes to zero.

Beyond the quantum scale, I believe that these results can be extended to cosmology in asking whether or not these infinities still persist in more complete cosmological models.

Reinforcement Learning

Can Machines Actually Learn?

It's been known for a while that large language models (LLMs) can be inconsistent in how far they can generalize beyond what they were trained on. The natural question arises: can AI actually generate meaningfully novel ideas, and what would be the mechanism that enables such creativity? My work in this area includes building world models and training an agent in such environments to understand causal structure (action A causes effect B). In this way, language is not the key architecture of emergent intelligence but rather its consequence. What then is needed for an agent to understand causality? Can an agent ever truly understand causal structure?

Making Models More Robust

The fact that one can improve a model's predictive ability by augmenting its training data feels like a trivial claim. The analogy would be that if you could train yourself to navigate mazes in the dark, then you could do a better job navigating mazes when the lights are turned back on. For generative models, this phenomenon has been empirically shown to exist, yet no universal mathematical framework has been established to describe it.

My work provides a mathematical theory for explaining this phenomenon, and as a result has developed methodological tools for selecting robust models. The mathematical framework revolves around generalized linear models, and is inspired by information thermodynamics. Beyond the aesthetics, thermodynamics has demonstrated great utility in the development of machine learning theory. The next big question is whether thermodynamics actually plays a fundamental role in learning or if it will always just be a tool that we call upon.

Other Interests

AI Safety And Cybersecurity

In the advent of OpenAI-HuggingFace incident of summer 2026, it is clear now that we are entering a new age of cybersecurity. Attackers are no longer just humans, they can be swarms of AI agents that can code faster than any human can. How do we defend ourselves and our information from such attacks? I am leading a project that examines the behaviors of AI agent hackers in simulated environments. The results of our experiments can be used to exploit the inherent weaknesses of agentic attackers, as well as better understand how the landscape of cybersecurity must evolve.
