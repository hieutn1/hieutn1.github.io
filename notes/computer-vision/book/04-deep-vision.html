4  Deep Learning, Faces and Multiple Views

After the midterm the course changes gears. The lectures from March 5 to March 28 bring in deep learning, spend two lectures on faces (recognition, pose, aging, facial expressions and biometrics more generally) and then go back to geometry with binocular and multiview stereo, epipolar geometry, structured light and structure from motion, finishing with deep networks for segmentation (CS 585 schedule). That’s two quite different kinds of computer vision sitting side by side, one that learns everything from data and one that’s built on exact geometry, and the interesting thing about the field right now is how much the two have been mixing. 4.1 Learning From Images

4.1.1 From hand-made features to learned ones

Everything in the first three chapters was designed by hand. Somebody decided that normalized red and green are good for skin, that the second moments describe a blob’s shape, that the Sobel mask is a good derivative, and each of those choices took real insight. The trouble is that for most recognition problems nobody knows what the right features are. What makes a picture a picture of a dog, as opposed to a cat or a wolf, isn’t something you can write down as a formula, and decades of carefully engineered features only got so far.

The turning point was data. In 2009 Fei-Fei Li’s group released ImageNet, which grew to more than 14 million images labelled with thousands of categories, and from 2010 on it ran a yearly challenge on a 1000-class subset. In 2012 Krizhevsky, Sutskever and Hinton entered a convolutional neural network, now called AlexNet, trained on two gaming GPUs, and got a top-5 error of about 15%, against about 26% for the next best entry, which used hand-designed features. Within a couple of years basically every entry was a neural network, and by 2015 ResNet (He and colleagues) had pushed the error below the rough estimate of human performance on that benchmark. The lesson people took from it is that given enough labelled data and enough computing, it’s better to learn the features than to design them, and that’s what the rest of this section is about.

4.1.2 Convolution layers

A convolutional network is built mostly from one operation that we already know well, which is sliding a small mask over an image and computing a weighted sum at every position. That’s template matching from Section 1.3.2, or the Sobel filter from Section 3.3.1, with one big difference, which is that the weights in the mask aren’t chosen by us, they’re learned. A convolution layer takes an input with Cin channels (3 for a colour image, or many more deeper in the network) and produces Cout output channels, each one the sum over input channels of a filtered version of that channel plus a bias,

\[ y_o(i, j) = b_o + \sum_{c=1}^{C_{\text{in}}} \sum_{m=0}^{k-1}\sum_{n=0}^{k-1} w_{o,c}(m, n)\, x_c(i + m,\, j + n). \]

(Deep learning libraries call this convolution even though it’s really correlation, since the mask isn’t flipped, and as the weights are learned anyway it makes no difference.) After the convolution comes a simple nonlinearity, nowadays almost always the ReLU, max(0, y), and every so often a pooling layer that shrinks the image, for example by keeping the maximum of each 2 × 2 block. Without the nonlinearity, a stack of convolutions would just be one big convolution, so the ReLU is what lets a deep network compute something more interesting than a linear filter.

Two properties make convolution layers a really good fit for images. They’re local, since each output only looks at a small neighbourhood, and they share weights, since the same mask is used at every position, so a feature detector that’s learned to find an eye in one corner of the image finds it everywhere else too. That makes the number of parameters tiny compared with a fully connected layer. A layer from 64 channels to 64 channels with 3 × 3 masks has 64 · 64 · 9 + 64 = 36,928 parameters, no matter how big the image is, while a fully connected layer between two 64-channel images of size 56 × 56 would need about 4 × 1010. As layers are stacked and the image is pooled down, each unit sees a bigger and bigger part of the original picture, and the learned filters go from edge and colour detectors in the first layer (they look a lot like the Gabor-like and centre-surround masks of Section 3.3.2) to parts of objects and then whole objects deeper in, which is something people have checked by visualizing what makes each unit respond.

The size of the output is a little formula that’s worth knowing. With input width W, mask size k, zero padding P on each side and stride S (how far the mask jumps between positions), the output width is

\[ W_{\text{out}} = \Big\lfloor \frac{W - k + 2P}{S} \Big\rfloor + 1 , \]

so a 3 × 3 mask with padding 1 and stride 1 keeps the size the same, which is the most common choice.

4.1.3 Training

A network for classification ends with one number per class, called the logits s1, …, sK, which get turned into probabilities by the softmax,

\[ p_k = \frac{e^{s_k}}{\sum_j e^{s_j}} , \]

and if the correct class is y, the loss for this example is the cross-entropy L = −log py, which is zero when the network puts all its probability on the right answer and grows without bound when it’s confidently wrong. Training means adjusting all the weights to make the average loss over the training images small, and it’s done by gradient descent, w ← w − η ∂L/∂w, with some learning rate η, using small random batches of images at a time (that’s stochastic gradient descent).

The gradients are computed by the chain rule, applied backwards through the layers, which is called backpropagation, and the very first step of it, the gradient of the loss with respect to the logits, has a really clean form that’s worth deriving. Write the loss as \(L = -s_y + \log\sum_j e^{s_j}\) and differentiate,

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial s_k} = -[k = y] + \frac{e^{s_k}}{\sum_j e^{s_j}} = p_k - [k = y], \]

so the gradient is just the predicted probabilities minus the one-hot vector of the correct answer. If the network gives the right class probability 0.7, the gradient pushes that logit up by 0.3 and pushes every other logit down by its own probability. Everything else in backpropagation is the same kind of bookkeeping, layer by layer, and libraries like PyTorch do it automatically.

Here’s the whole process in NumPy, on a toy problem, where we train a small network to tell apart 8 × 8 images of horizontal bars, vertical bars and diagonal bars with noise added. The network has one hidden layer of 32 ReLU units, which is a fully connected network rather than a convolutional one, but the training loop is exactly the same idea.

def bar_images(n, rng):
    X = np.zeros((n, 8, 8)); y = rng.integers(0, 3, n)
    for i in range(n):
        k = rng.integers(1, 7)
        if y[i] == 0: X[i, k, :] = 1
        elif y[i] == 1: X[i, :, k] = 1
        else: X[i] = np.eye(8, k=k - 3)
    return (X + rng.normal(0, 0.5, X.shape)).reshape(n, 64), y

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
Xtr, ytr = bar_images(3000, rng); Xte, yte = bar_images(500, rng)
W1 = rng.normal(0, 0.1, (64, 32)); b1 = np.zeros(32); W2 = rng.normal(0, 0.1, (32, 3)); b2 = np.zeros(3)
losses, accs = [], []
for step in range(600):
    idx = rng.integers(0, len(Xtr), 64)
    X, y = Xtr[idx], ytr[idx]
    h = np.maximum(0, X @ W1 + b1)                      # forward pass
    s = h @ W2 + b2
    p = np.exp(s - s.max(1, keepdims=True)); p /= p.sum(1, keepdims=True)
    losses.append(-np.log(p[np.arange(64), y]).mean())
    ds = p.copy(); ds[np.arange(64), y] -= 1; ds /= 64   # softmax cross-entropy gradient: p - onehot
    dW2, db2 = h.T @ ds, ds.sum(0)                       # backward pass, by the chain rule
    dh = ds @ W2.T; dh[h <= 0] = 0
    dW1, db1 = X.T @ dh, dh.sum(0)
    for P, dP in [(W1, dW1), (b1, db1), (W2, dW2), (b2, db2)]:
        P -= 0.1 * dP
    if step % 20 == 0:
        accs.append((step, np.mean(np.argmax(np.maximum(0, Xte @ W1 + b1) @ W2 + b2, 1) == yte)))
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.5, 3.2)); ax2 = ax.twinx()
ax.plot(losses, color="#3B6FB6", lw=0.8, label="training loss")
ax2.plot(*zip(*accs), color="#E07A3F", label="test accuracy")
ax.set_xlabel("step"); ax.set_ylabel("loss"); ax2.set_ylabel("accuracy"); ax2.set_ylim(0, 1.02)
fig.legend(loc="center right", fontsize=8); fig.tight_layout(); plt.show()
print("final test accuracy:", accs[-1][1])
final test accuracy: 0.936

The same thing in PyTorch, for a real convolutional network on real images, looks like this (it isn’t run here, since the browser can’t load PyTorch, but it runs as it is on your own computer with torchvision installed):

import torch, torch.nn as nn, torchvision
net = nn.Sequential(
    nn.Conv2d(3, 32, 3, padding=1), nn.ReLU(), nn.MaxPool2d(2),
    nn.Conv2d(32, 64, 3, padding=1), nn.ReLU(), nn.MaxPool2d(2),
    nn.Flatten(), nn.Linear(64 * 8 * 8, 10))
data = torchvision.datasets.CIFAR10(".", train=True, download=True,
                                    transform=torchvision.transforms.ToTensor())
loader = torch.utils.data.DataLoader(data, batch_size=64, shuffle=True)
opt = torch.optim.SGD(net.parameters(), lr=0.05, momentum=0.9)
for epoch in range(5):
    for images, labels in loader:
        loss = nn.functional.cross_entropy(net(images), labels)
        opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()     # backward() is backpropagation

In practice almost nobody trains a vision network from scratch for a new problem. The standard move is transfer learning: take a network that was trained on ImageNet, keep all its layers except the last one, and retrain only that last layer (or fine-tune everything with a small learning rate) on your own, much smaller dataset. The early layers learned general features like edges and textures that are useful for almost any picture, so a few hundred labelled images can be enough, which is how most of the course projects end up working.

4.2 Faces

4.2.1 Detection, verification and identification

Faces get two whole lectures, and there’s a good reason, since face recognition is probably the most widely deployed (and most argued about) application of computer vision. It helps to separate three tasks that often get lumped together. Detection finds the faces in a picture, which is what Chapter 1 did with skin colour and templates, and which modern detectors do with a network that outputs boxes. Verification asks whether two face images show the same person, a yes or no question, which is what unlocking a phone does. Identification asks who a face is, by comparing it against a gallery of known people, which is what a search through a database of photos does.

The way modern systems do both verification and identification is through embeddings. A network maps each aligned face crop to a vector, often 512 numbers long and normalized to unit length, and it’s trained so that two photos of the same person map to nearby vectors while photos of different people end up far apart. Comparing faces then just means comparing vectors, usually by cosine similarity, which for unit vectors is the dot product, and that’s nice because the network never needs to have seen the people it’s comparing. Training uses huge collections of face photos labelled by identity, and a lot of the research in this area has been about the loss function, from triplet losses (pull an anchor towards a photo of the same person and push it away from a photo of somebody else, as in Google’s FaceNet) to losses like ArcFace that add an angular margin to the softmax of Section 4.1.3 so the classes are forced further apart.

The lecture’s reading list is also a tour of the datasets that drove the field, and they’re worth knowing because each one was made to expose a weakness of the methods before it. Labeled Faces in the Wild (LFW, 2007) has about 13,000 photos of 5,749 people collected from news sites, and it was the standard verification benchmark until methods passed 99% on it. MegaFace (2016) tested identification against a million distractors, FaceScrub provided clean photos of celebrities, IARPA Janus Benchmark A (IJB-A, 2015) brought in much harder poses and image quality, CFP (Celebrities in Frontal-Profile, 2016) paired frontal with profile views, and AFLW2000 is used for aligning faces at large poses. VGGFace2 (Cao and colleagues, 2018) collected millions of images with lots of variation in pose and age for training.

4.2.2 Measuring a verifier

A verifier compares the similarity score of two faces with a threshold, and there are two ways for it to be wrong. It can reject a genuine pair (a false non-match, which is annoying) or accept an impostor pair (a false match, which is a security problem). Moving the threshold trades one for the other, and the honest way to describe a verifier is to show the whole trade-off. If we compute scores for many genuine pairs and many impostor pairs, then for each threshold τ we get a false match rate FMR(τ), the fraction of impostor scores at or above τ, and a false non-match rate FNMR(τ), the fraction of genuine scores below it. Plotting the true match rate 1 − FNMR against FMR as τ varies gives the ROC curve (receiver operating characteristic), which Fawcett’s tutorial in the reading list covers carefully. Two summary numbers are common. The equal error rate is where FMR = FNMR, and systems for security are usually quoted as a true match rate at a fixed, very small false match rate, like 10−4, since that’s the regime where they’re used.

4.2.3 Pose and aging

The March 19 lecture picks up the two things that make face recognition hard even for good networks. The first is pose. A profile view shows a completely different set of pixels from a frontal view, and half the face is hidden, so matching a profile against a frontal photo is much harder than matching two frontal ones, which is what the CFP benchmark was built to measure. A common approach, which the papers by Zhu and colleagues in the reading list develop, is to fit a 3D face model to the photo, estimate the pose, and then either render the face from the front or align landmarks in 3D, which brings the image formation of Section 2.1.2 right back into a deep learning pipeline. The second is aging. Faces change over years, especially for children, and the question of how long a face photo stays useful for recognition was studied in a longitudinal analysis by Best-Rowden and Jain (2017), using repeated mugshots of the same people over many years, which found that genuine similarity scores drop slowly with the time between photos, and that for most people recognition stays reliable over several years but not indefinitely.

4.2.4 Facial expressions

Recognizing what a face is doing, rather than whose it is, has a nice classical solution in the reading list, the paper by Black and Yacoob (1997) on recognizing facial expressions from image motion. Their idea was to describe the motion of each part of the face with a small parametric model rather than a free flow field. The face as a whole moves roughly like a plane, and each eye, eyebrow and the mouth gets its own model with a few parameters, the simplest of which is the affine flow

\[ u(x, y) = a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 y, \qquad v(x, y) = a_3 + a_4 x + a_5 y , \]

with (x, y) measured from the centre of the region. Six numbers capture translation, rotation, scaling and shearing of the region, and Black and Yacoob added a curvature term for the mouth and eyebrows. The parameters are estimated robustly from the image derivatives, using the same brightness constancy constraint as Section 3.5.1 but with the flow replaced by the model, and then, for each frame, they’re turned into simple descriptions like “the mouth is stretching horizontally” or “the brows are rising”, and a smile or surprise is recognized from the sequence of these descriptions over time (onset, apex, offset). It’s a nice example of how a little bit of structure can make motion much easier to interpret than a raw flow field, and of the aperture problem disappearing, since six parameters for a whole region are very well determined even though each pixel’s flow isn’t.

4.2.5 Biometrics

Face recognition is one kind of biometric, a way of recognizing people from their bodies or behaviour, along with fingerprints, iris patterns, voice, gait and others. They all share the verification framework of Section 4.2.2, with genuine and impostor score distributions and a threshold, and they differ in how distinctive and how permanent the trait is and how easy it is to collect, which is where faces win, since a camera can capture them at a distance without anyone’s cooperation. That same convenience is what makes face recognition so contested. It can be used for surveillance that people never agreed to, and evaluations like NIST’s Face Recognition Vendor Test have measured that many algorithms have different error rates for different demographic groups, which matters a lot when a false match can mean a wrongful arrest. We’ll come back to this in Section 5.8, and it’s worth keeping in mind while working through the methods, since the error rates in an ROC curve turn into real people’s experiences once a system is deployed.

4.3 Stereo And Multiple Views

In Section 2.4 the two cameras were side by side and pointing the same way, which made matches lie on the same row. Real camera pairs aren’t arranged that neatly, and in structure from motion the “second camera” is just the same camera a moment later, somewhere else, pointing somewhere else. This section is about the geometry that ties two arbitrary views together, which is beautiful and surprisingly compact.

4.3.1 Epipolar geometry

Take two pinhole cameras with centres O1 and O2 and a scene point P. The three points O1, O2 and P span a plane, the epipolar plane, and the image of P in each camera has to lie where that plane cuts the image, which is a line, the epipolar line. So if we know where P appears in the first image, its match in the second image has to lie on a known line, and the search for correspondences is one-dimensional again, just along a slanted line instead of a row. All the epipolar lines in one image pass through one point, the epipole, which is the image of the other camera’s centre.

To turn this into algebra, work in the first camera’s coordinates, and let the second camera be rotated by R and translated by t, so a point P1 in the first camera’s coordinates is P2 = R P1 + t in the second camera’s. Let x1 and x2 be the image points as homogeneous 3-vectors in normalized coordinates (that is, after removing the intrinsics, x = K−1(u, v, 1)⊤), so P1 = Z1x1 and P2 = Z2x2 for the unknown depths. The three vectors t, Rx1 and x2, all seen in the second camera’s frame, lie in the epipolar plane, so the triple product is zero,

\[ x_2^\top\big(t \times R\,x_1\big) = 0 . \]

Writing the cross product with t as multiplication by the skew-symmetric matrix [t]×, this becomes

\[ x_2^\top E\, x_1 = 0, \qquad E = [t]_\times R, \qquad [t]_\times = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -t_3 & t_2 \\ t_3 & 0 & -t_1 \\ -t_2 & t_1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} . \]

E is the essential matrix (Longuet-Higgins, 1981), and it packs the whole relative pose of the two cameras into nine numbers, defined up to scale. Since [t]× has rank 2, so does E, and for a point x1 in the first image, the vector E x1 is exactly the epipolar line in the second image in the homogeneous line notation of Section 2.2.2. If we work in pixel coordinates instead, the same constraint holds with the fundamental matrix F = K2−⊤ E K1−1,

\[ \tilde{x}_2^\top F\, \tilde{x}_1 = 0 , \]

where x̃ are homogeneous pixel coordinates. F also has rank 2 and is defined up to scale, so it has 7 degrees of freedom, and the great thing about it is that it can be estimated from point matches alone, without knowing anything about the cameras.

4.3.2 The eight-point algorithm

Each match (x̃1, x̃2) gives one equation x̃2⊤ F x̃1 = 0, and this equation is linear in the nine entries of F. Writing out the product, with x̃1 = (u1, v1, 1) and x̃2 = (u2, v2, 1), it’s

\[ \big(u_2 u_1,\; u_2 v_1,\; u_2,\; v_2 u_1,\; v_2 v_1,\; v_2,\; u_1,\; v_1,\; 1\big) \cdot f = 0 , \]

where f lists the entries of F row by row. Eight matches give eight such rows, which (with F only defined up to scale) is enough to pin it down, and with more matches we solve the least squares problem with ‖f‖ = 1 by taking the right singular vector with the smallest singular value, exactly as for the vanishing point in Section 2.2.2. The estimate won’t quite have rank 2 because of noise, so the last step forces it, by taking the SVD of F, setting the smallest singular value to zero and multiplying back.

There’s one more step that matters a lot in practice, which Hartley pointed out in a 1997 paper with the memorable title “In defense of the eight-point algorithm”. Pixel coordinates are numbers in the hundreds, so the entries of each row range from about 1 to about 105, and that makes the least squares problem badly conditioned. The fix is to normalize first. Translate each image’s points so their centroid is at the origin and scale them so their average distance from it is √2, run the algorithm on the normalized points, and undo the normalization at the end. If T1 and T2 are the normalizing transforms (3 × 3 matrices acting on homogeneous points) and F̂ is the matrix found for the normalized points, then F = T2⊤ F̂ T1. This small change makes the results dramatically more accurate, and it’s a good example of how much numerical care matters in geometry.

4.3.3 Triangulation

Once we know the two camera matrices P1 = K1[I | 0] and P2 = K2[R | t] and a match, getting the 3D point back is called triangulation. In a perfect world the two rays through the matched pixels meet at P, but with noise they miss each other slightly, so again we solve a small least squares problem. For each camera, the projection says that the homogeneous image point x̃ is parallel to PiX, where X is the homogeneous 3D point, so x̃ × (PiX) = 0. Two of the three components of this cross product are independent, and writing pik⊤ for the k-th row of Pi, they’re

\[ u\,(p^{3\top} X) - p^{1\top} X = 0, \qquad v\,(p^{3\top} X) - p^{2\top} X = 0 . \]

Two cameras give four equations in the four entries of X, which is again a homogeneous least squares problem solved with the SVD, and then we divide by the last entry. This is the linear or DLT method, and it’s what cv2.triangulatePoints does. It doesn’t minimize the actual pixel errors, so more careful methods refine it, but it’s a very good start. And it’s the general version of the depth formula Z = fB/d from Section 2.4.1, which you can check by plugging in the side-by-side cameras.

4.3.4 Rectification and multiview stereo

Searching along slanted epipolar lines is awkward, so stereo systems usually rectify the images first, warping each one with a homography (a 3 × 3 matrix acting on homogeneous pixel coordinates) so that the epipolar lines become horizontal rows, and the side-by-side geometry of Section 2.4 applies, including block matching along rows and Z = fB/d. OpenCV does this with cv2.stereoRectify once the cameras are calibrated. With more than two cameras, multiview stereo combines the evidence from all of them, for example by testing, for each pixel of a reference image, a range of depths and checking how consistent the colours are in all the other views at the projected positions (the plane-sweep idea), and the best systems now produce dense 3D models of whole buildings from collections of photos.

4.3.5 Active stereo and structured light

Stereo matching fails on blank surfaces because there’s nothing to match, so an obvious trick is to put the texture there ourselves. Active stereo replaces one of the cameras with a projector, or adds a projector to a stereo pair, and shines a known pattern onto the scene. The projector is geometrically just a camera running backwards, so the triangulation of Section 4.3.3 works unchanged, as long as we know which projector pixel lit each camera pixel. The simplest version sweeps a single laser stripe across the object, so each image has one bright curve whose position along each row gives depth, which is how a lot of industrial 3D scanners work. Faster systems project a sequence of binary stripe patterns, so that the on/off sequence a camera pixel sees over, say, 10 patterns spells out the projector column in binary (210 = 1024 columns from 10 images). The patterns are usually Gray codes, an ordering of binary numbers in which consecutive numbers differ in only one bit, so a pixel right at the boundary between two stripes can only be off by one column instead of being completely wrong. The first Kinect (2010) used a single fixed pattern of infrared speckles, matched against a stored reference image, which let it measure depth at video rate.

4.3.6 Structure from motion

Now suppose we have just one camera, moving through a static scene, and we track some points through many frames with something like the Lucas-Kanade tracker of Section 3.5.4. Can we get back both the 3D shape of the scene and the motion of the camera? That’s structure from motion, and under the weak perspective model of Section 2.1.3 there’s a lovely answer, the factorization method of Tomasi and Kanade (1992).

Under orthographic projection (weak perspective with the scale folded in), the image of point j in frame i is uij = mi · Sj + ai and vij = ni · Sj + bi, where Sj is the 3D point, mi and ni are the first two rows of the camera’s rotation in frame i, and (ai, bi) is a translation. If we measure every point relative to the centroid of all points in each frame, the translations drop out (as long as we put the world origin at the centroid of the 3D points). Now stack all the measurements of F frames and P points into one big 2F × P measurement matrix W, with the u’s of frame i in row i and the v’s in row F + i. Then

\[ W = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} m_1^\top \\ \vdots \\ m_F^\top \\ n_1^\top \\ \vdots \\ n_F^\top \end{pmatrix}}_{M\ (2F \times 3)} \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} S_1 & \cdots & S_P \end{pmatrix}}_{S\ (3 \times P)} , \]

so W, however big, has rank at most 3. That’s the whole insight. Take the SVD of W, keep the three largest singular values, W ≈ U3Σ3V3⊤, and set M̂ = U3Σ31/2 and Ŝ = Σ31/2V3⊤. This recovers motion and shape, but only up to an unknown invertible 3 × 3 matrix A, since M̂A and A−1Ŝ multiply to the same W. To pin A down Tomasi and Kanade use the fact that each mi and ni must be unit vectors and perpendicular to each other, which gives linear equations for the symmetric matrix AA⊤, and then A follows from a Cholesky factorization (up to a rotation of the whole scene, which really can’t be recovered, since you can’t tell the world’s orientation from images alone). The truncated SVD also gives the best rank-3 fit to noisy data, so noise is averaged over all frames and points at once.

For perspective cameras, the modern approach is incremental. Estimate the relative pose of two views from the essential matrix of Section 4.3.1, triangulate points, add more views one at a time, and then refine everything together with bundle adjustment, a big nonlinear least squares problem over all camera poses and 3D points that minimizes the total reprojection error. That’s how systems like COLMAP build 3D models from thousands of tourist photos.

4.4 Segmentation With Deep Networks

The last lecture in this block comes back to segmentation, which in Chapter 1 meant thresholding and connected components, and asks what deep networks do with it. There are a few flavours. Semantic segmentation labels every pixel with a class (road, car, person, sky), instance segmentation also separates individual objects (this car versus that car), and medical image segmentation, which the course returns to often, usually means outlining one organ or tumour precisely.

The first big step was the fully convolutional network of Long, Shelhamer and Darrell (2015), who noticed that a classification network with its final fully connected layers replaced by convolutions produces a coarse map of class scores rather than one score per image, and that this map can be upsampled back to the original resolution and trained pixel by pixel with the cross-entropy loss of Section 4.1.3. The problem is that the pooling layers throw away fine detail, so boundaries come out blurry, and the fix that stuck was U-Net (Ronneberger, Fischer and Brox, 2015), designed for biomedical images. It has a contracting path that pools down, like a classifier, and an expanding path that upsamples back up, and at each resolution it copies the features from the contracting path across to the expanding path (the skip connections), so the decoder gets both the coarse “what” from deep layers and the precise “where” from shallow ones. U-Net works well with surprisingly few training images, which is why it’s still a default in medical imaging. For instance segmentation, Mask R-CNN (He and colleagues, 2017) adds a small mask-predicting branch to an object detector, and more recently, models like Meta’s Segment Anything (2023) were trained on a billion masks and can segment almost any object from a click or a box.

To measure how good a segmentation is, we compare the predicted mask A with the ground-truth mask B. The two standard scores are the intersection over union (or Jaccard index) and the Dice coefficient,

\[ \text{IoU} = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|}, \qquad \text{Dice} = \frac{2|A\cap B|}{|A| + |B|} . \]

They’re closely related. Using |A| + |B| = |A ∪ B| + |A ∩ B| and dividing top and bottom by |A ∪ B|, Dice = 2 IoU/(1 + IoU), so they always rank segmentations the same way, but Dice is always the larger of the two (except at 0 and 1), which is worth remembering when comparing numbers across papers that use different ones. Both ignore true negatives, which is the right thing to do when the object is small compared with the background, since a method that labels everything as background would otherwise look very accurate.
