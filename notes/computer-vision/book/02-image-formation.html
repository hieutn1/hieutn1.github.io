2  Image Formation

In the first chapter an image was just an array that showed up from somewhere, and we never really asked where the numbers came from. This chapter is about that question, which is how a three-dimensional world turns into a flat picture, and it follows the two lectures on image formation in the course schedule, the one on the pinhole model and binocular stereo and the one on vanishing points, the lens equation and computer vision for paintings (CS 585 schedule). The geometry here is old, and painters had worked most of it out by the fifteenth century, but it’s the thing every later chapter quietly assumes, so it’s worth getting it straight once and for all. The main references are Chapter 2 of Horn’s Robot Vision and Chapter 2 of Szeliski’s book (Szeliski, 2nd ed., 2022).

2.1 The Pinhole Camera

2.1.1 Perspective projection

Take a box, make it light-tight, and poke a tiny hole in one side. Light from a point in the scene can only get into the box along one line, the one through the hole, so each scene point lights up exactly one spot on the back wall, and the back wall ends up showing an upside-down picture of the world. That’s the pinhole camera (or camera obscura, which people were using to watch eclipses safely long before anybody thought of film), and it’s still the model that almost all of computer vision is built on, even though real cameras have lenses.

Let’s set up coordinates. Put the pinhole at the origin, let the Z axis point out into the scene along the optical axis, and put the back wall, the image plane, at distance f behind the hole, where f is called the focal length. A scene point P = (X, Y, Z) sends its ray through the origin, and the ray keeps going until it hits the image plane at Z = −f. Since the ray is a straight line through the origin, the image point is just P scaled by some factor, and the factor has to be −f/Z to land on the plane, so the image point is (−fX/Z, −fY/Z). The minus signs are the upside-down part. Nobody wants to carry them around, so it’s standard to pretend the image plane sits at distance f in front of the pinhole instead (a virtual image plane, which you can’t build but which gives the same picture right side up), and then

\[ x = f\,\frac{X}{Z}, \qquad y = f\,\frac{Y}{Z}. \]

These are the equations of perspective projection, and pretty much everything in this chapter is a consequence of the division by Z. You can also get them from similar triangles, if you draw the ray, the optical axis and the perpendicular from P to the axis, which is how Horn does it in Chapter 2, but the scaling argument is the same thing in one line.

The division by Z is what makes far things look small. An object of height H at distance Z has an image of height fH/Z, so doubling the distance halves the image, and it’s also why the projection loses information, because every point along the ray, (tX, tY, tZ) for any t > 0, lands on the same image point. A single picture can’t tell a small nearby object from a big far one, and getting depth back is what the second half of this chapter is about.

cube = np.array([[x, y, z] for x in (-1, 1) for y in (-1, 1) for z in (0, 2)], float)
edges = [(i, j) for i in range(8) for j in range(i + 1, 8) if np.sum(cube[i] != cube[j]) == 1]

def pinhole(P, f=1.0):
    return f * P[:, :2] / P[:, 2:3]

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(8, 3.6))

2.1.2 From the image plane to pixels

The equations above give positions on the image plane in the same units as the scene, but what we actually get from a camera is pixel coordinates, counted from the top-left corner like in Section 1.1.3. Two more things happen on the way. The sensor has some number of pixels per millimetre, so the coordinates get scaled, and the pixel grid starts at a corner instead of at the point where the optical axis hits the sensor (the principal point), so the coordinates get shifted. Putting both in,

\[ u = f_x\,\frac{X}{Z} + c_x, \qquad v = f_y\,\frac{Y}{Z} + c_y, \]

where fx and fy are the focal length measured in pixels, which are equal when the pixels are square and (cx, cy) is the principal point in pixels, usually somewhere near the centre of the image.

The division by Z is annoying to carry around, because it’s the only nonlinear part, and there’s a really neat trick that hides it, which is homogeneous coordinates. We represent the image point (u, v) by any triple (w u, w v, w) with w ≠ 0, so (u, v, 1), (2u, 2v, 2) and so on all mean the same point, and to get back to ordinary coordinates we divide by the last entry. With that convention the projection becomes a plain matrix multiplication,

\[ \begin{pmatrix} w\,u \\ w\,v \\ w \end{pmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} f_x & 0 & c_x \\ 0 & f_y & c_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}}_{K} \begin{pmatrix} X \\ Y \\ Z \end{pmatrix}, \]

and you can check that w comes out as Z, so dividing by it does exactly the perspective division. The matrix K is called the intrinsic matrix (or calibration matrix), because it only depends on the camera itself.

So far the scene points were given in the camera’s own coordinate system. Usually they’re given in some world coordinate system instead, say with the origin on the floor of the room, and then we first have to move them into camera coordinates with a rotation R and a translation t, Pcam = R Pworld + t. Writing the world point as (X, Y, Z, 1) in homogeneous coordinates turns that into a matrix product too, and the whole camera becomes one 3 × 4 matrix,

\[ \begin{pmatrix} w\,u \\ w\,v \\ w \end{pmatrix} = K \begin{pmatrix} R & t \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} X \\ Y \\ Z \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} . \]

R and t are the extrinsic parameters, since they describe where the camera is rather than what kind of camera it is. Finding K for a real camera is called calibration, and the usual way to do it is to photograph a checkerboard from several angles and solve for the parameters that best explain where the corners show up, which OpenCV does with cv2.calibrateCamera.

2.1.3 When perspective hardly matters

Look again at the far cube in the figure above. When an object’s depth range is small compared with its distance, Z barely changes across it, and we can replace every Z by one average depth Z0,

\[ x \approx \frac{f}{Z_0}\,X, \qquad y \approx \frac{f}{Z_0}\,Y . \]

That’s weak perspective (or scaled orthographic projection), and it’s just a uniform scaling of X and Y, so parallel lines stay parallel and the whole thing is linear. How good is it? If a point sits at depth Z = Z0 + ΔZ, the exact projection is fX/(Z0 + ΔZ) = (fX/Z0)(1 − ΔZ/Z0 + …), so the relative error is about ΔZ/Z0. A face 10 cm deep seen from 2 metres gives errors around 5%, and a building seen from a plane gives errors you’d never notice. A lot of algorithms are much easier under weak perspective, which is why it keeps showing up, for example in the factorization method for structure from motion in Section 4.3.6.

2.2 Vanishing Points

2.2.1 Where parallel lines meet

Stand in the middle of a straight railway track and the two rails seem to meet at a point on the horizon, even though they never actually meet. That point is a vanishing point, and it’s one of those things that’s obvious once you see it but kind of fun to derive. Take a line in the scene through the point A = (AX, AY, AZ) with direction D = (DX, DY, DZ), so its points are A + sD for all s. The image of the point with parameter s is

\[ x(s) = f\,\frac{A_X + s D_X}{A_Z + s D_Z}, \qquad y(s) = f\,\frac{A_Y + s D_Y}{A_Z + s D_Z}, \]

and as s goes to infinity (walking off along the line, away from the camera, which needs DZ > 0) both fractions go to the ratio of the s terms,

\[ x_\infty = f\,\frac{D_X}{D_Z}, \qquad y_\infty = f\,\frac{D_Y}{D_Z}. \]

The starting point A has dropped out completely, so every line with direction D, no matter where it is, heads towards the same image point, and that’s the vanishing point for the direction D. Two consequences are worth writing down. Lines parallel to the image plane (DZ = 0) have no vanishing point, or rather it’s at infinity, so they stay parallel in the picture, which is why the vertical edges of a building photographed straight on stay vertical. And the vanishing point of D is exactly where the ray from the pinhole in direction D hits the image plane, so it only depends on the direction, which is the geometric reason for everything above. All directions parallel to the ground plane give vanishing points on one line in the image, the horizon, which is the image of the plane through the pinhole parallel to the ground, which is why the horizon always sits at the height of your eyes.

2.2.2 Lines and points in homogeneous coordinates

To find a vanishing point in a real photograph we need to intersect lines, and homogeneous coordinates make that pretty painless. A line in the image, a x + b y + c = 0, can be stored as the triple ℓ = (a, b, c), and a point p = (x, y, 1) lies on it exactly when ℓ · p = 0. Now take two points p1 and p2. The cross product p1 × p2 is perpendicular to both, so it has zero dot product with both, which means it’s the line through them. And by the same argument, the cross product of two lines ℓ1 × ℓ2 is the point on both, so it’s their intersection. Points and lines play completely symmetric roles here, and parallel lines aren’t a special case at all, their intersection just comes out with a last coordinate of zero, which is a point at infinity.

In a real photo, though, three or more lines that should meet at one vanishing point never quite do, because the edges we detect are noisy. So instead of intersecting two of them we look for the point that comes closest to lying on all of them. If we normalize each line so that a² + b² = 1, then ℓ · p is the signed distance from p to the line, and we want the homogeneous p that makes the sum of squared ℓi · p as small as possible. Stack the lines as the rows of a matrix L, and we’re minimizing ‖L p‖² over p with ‖p‖ = 1 (the scale of p doesn’t matter, so we have to fix it somehow), and the answer is the right singular vector of L with the smallest singular value. That’s the standard least squares with a unit-norm constraint trick, and it’ll come back in the eight-point algorithm of Section 4.3.2.

(Strictly speaking, ℓ · p is only the distance when p has last coordinate 1, so minimizing ‖Lp‖ with ‖p‖ = 1 isn’t exactly the geometric least squares problem, but it’s very close when the vanishing point is at a sensible distance and it’s what almost everybody uses.)

2.2.3 Focal length from vanishing points

Here’s a lovely application. Suppose a photo shows a building, and we find the vanishing points v1 and v2 (in pixels) of two horizontal directions that we know are perpendicular in the scene, like the two walls of the building. From Section 2.2.1 the vanishing point of direction D is the image of the ray in direction D, so with the principal point c = (cx, cy) and focal length f in pixels, the directions are proportional to (v1 − c, f) and (v2 − c, f), where we’ve glued the third coordinate f onto the two-dimensional offset. These two directions are perpendicular, so their dot product vanishes,

\[ (v_1 - c)\cdot(v_2 - c) + f^2 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f = \sqrt{-(v_1 - c)\cdot(v_2 - c)} . \]

So two vanishing points of perpendicular directions, plus a guess for the principal point (usually just the image centre), are enough to work out the focal length of a camera you’ve never seen, from a single photo. With three mutually perpendicular directions you can even get the principal point too, since it turns out to be the orthocentre of the triangle formed by the three vanishing points, which is a classic result, from Caprile and Torre’s 1990 paper on calibration with vanishing points. It also explains a fact photographers know, which is that a wide-angle lens (small f) puts the vanishing points close together and close to the image, so you get dramatic perspective, while a telephoto lens pushes them way out and flattens everything.

2.2.4 Computer vision and paintings

The second image formation lecture spends some time on paintings, and it’s a nice detour. Linear perspective was worked out by Renaissance artists, with Brunelleschi’s famous demonstration in Florence around 1415 (he painted the Baptistery on a panel, drilled a hole in it and had people look through the hole at a mirror, so they could compare painting and building directly) and Alberti’s 1435 treatise On Painting, which describes the picture as a window through which you look at the scene, which is exactly our virtual image plane. A painter who follows these rules is basically computing perspective projection by hand, and that means we can run the analysis of this section backwards on a painting.

For example, if a painting shows a tiled floor, the lines of the tiles that are parallel in the scene should all meet at one vanishing point, and we can check with the least squares fit of Section 2.2.2 how well they do. If they meet nicely, the painter was using a perspective construction or an optical device, and if they wander around, the perspective was done by eye. Using the vanishing points of a floor and the horizon you can also measure relative heights of people in the painting, which is the idea of single-view metrology (Criminisi, Reid and Zisserman wrote the standard paper on it in 2000, and they used paintings as examples). This kind of analysis has been used in arguments among art historians, most famously around David Hockney’s claim that some old masters secretly traced projections from lenses and mirrors, which David Stork and others tested by checking exactly these geometric constraints. I won’t try to settle that argument here, but it’s a fun example of the geometry being useful well outside engineering.

2.3 Lenses

2.3.1 Why not just a pinhole?

A pinhole camera has a real problem, which is that it’s either dim or blurry. A smaller hole gives a sharper picture, because each scene point lights up a smaller spot, but it lets in less light, so the exposure takes forever, and if you make the hole really tiny, diffraction kicks in and the picture gets blurry again anyway. A lens solves this by collecting all the light that hits a big aperture from one scene point and bending it so that it comes back together at one image point, so we get both brightness and sharpness, at least for points at the right distance.

2.3.2 The thin lens equation

The simplest model of a lens is the thin lens, which we treat as a plane with two rules for how rays get bent. A ray through the centre of the lens goes straight through, and a ray coming in parallel to the optical axis gets bent so that it passes through the focal point, at distance f behind the lens. Now take a scene point at height h and distance zo in front of the lens and follow both rays. The central ray reaches the image side at height −h zi/zo at distance zi (similar triangles through the centre), and the parallel ray leaves the lens at height h and passes through the focal point, so on the image side it drops from h to 0 over distance f and keeps going, which puts it at height h(1 − zi/f) at distance zi. For the two rays to meet at the image point, their heights have to agree,

\[ -\frac{h\,z_i}{z_o} = h\Big(1 - \frac{z_i}{f}\Big) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{1}{z_o} + \frac{1}{z_i} = \frac{1}{f}, \]

and dividing by h zi and rearranging gives the thin lens equation. The height h dropped out, so every point at distance zo comes to focus at the same distance zi behind the lens, so a whole plane of the scene is in focus at once, and that’s the plane the camera is focused on. A few things follow right away. When zo goes to infinity, zi goes to f, so a camera focused at infinity has its sensor at the focal point, and as objects come closer the image moves further back, which is why focusing on something close means moving the lens away from the sensor. The magnification is zi/zo, and for distant objects that’s about f/zo, so far away the lens acts exactly like a pinhole at its centre with “focal length” f, which is why the pinhole model works so well. And when zo = f, zi is infinite, so a point at the focal distance in front of the lens can’t be focused at all (its rays come out parallel, which is how a flashlight works).

2.3.3 Blur and depth of field

What about scene points that aren’t at the focused distance? Say the sensor is at distance s behind the lens, which focuses points at distance zo = (1/f − 1/s)−1 perfectly, and a point at some other distance z focuses at z’ = (1/f − 1/z)−1 instead. The cone of light from the lens aperture, of diameter D, converges to a point at z’ and then the sensor at s cuts the cone, giving a disc of light, the blur circle. Similar triangles in the cone give its diameter,

\[ b = D\,\frac{|s - z'|}{z'} . \]

A blur circle smaller than a pixel is invisible, so there’s a whole range of distances that look sharp, the depth of field. The formula shows how photographers control it. Making the aperture D smaller makes b smaller in proportion, so a small aperture (a large f-number N = f/D, like f/16) gives a deep depth of field, and a wide aperture (like f/1.8) gives the blurry backgrounds people like in portraits. It also says that blur carries depth information, since b depends on z, and there are methods that estimate depth from how blurry things are (depth from defocus), though we won’t go into them here.

2.4 Two Eyes

2.4.1 Depth from disparity

A single image throws depth away, as we saw in Section 2.1.1, but two images from slightly different places can get it back, which is what our two eyes do and what the binocular stereo lecture is about. The simplest arrangement has two identical pinhole cameras side by side, pointing in the same direction, with their pinholes a distance B apart along the x axis (B is called the baseline). Put the left camera at the origin and the right one at (B, 0, 0). A scene point (X, Y, Z) projects to

\[ x_l = f\,\frac{X}{Z}, \qquad x_r = f\,\frac{X - B}{Z}, \qquad y_l = y_r = f\,\frac{Y}{Z}, \]

so the point shows up in the same row of both images but shifted horizontally by the disparity

\[ d = x_l - x_r = \frac{f B}{Z} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad Z = \frac{f B}{d} . \]

Depth is inversely proportional to disparity, so nearby things shift a lot between the two views and far things hardly move, which you can check by holding a finger in front of your face and closing one eye and then the other.

How accurate is this? Differentiate Z = fB/d with respect to d, and an error δd in the disparity gives

\[ \delta Z \approx \frac{f B}{d^2}\,\delta d = \frac{Z^2}{f B}\,\delta d , \]

so the depth error grows like the square of the distance. A stereo rig that measures depth to a millimetre at half a metre is off by about 40 centimetres at 10 metres, with the same disparity error, and the only ways to fight that are a longer baseline or a longer focal length (both of which make it harder to find matches, since the two views look less alike). That’s why the stereo cameras on cars have their lenses as far apart as the windshield allows.

2.4.2 Finding matches

All of this assumes we know which pixel in the right image goes with which pixel in the left image, the correspondence problem, and that’s by far the hard part. In our side-by-side arrangement the search is one-dimensional, since the match of a left pixel has to be in the same row, somewhere to its left in the right image. The simplest method reuses template matching from Section 1.3.2. Take a small window around the left pixel, slide it along the same row of the right image over a range of disparities, and pick the disparity with the smallest SSD (or the largest normalized correlation). This is called block matching, and it works well on textured surfaces and badly on blank walls, where every window looks the same, which is the aperture problem in another form (we’ll meet it properly with optical flow in Section 3.5.2).

A classic way to test a stereo algorithm is a random-dot stereogram, which Béla Julesz used in the 1960s to show that human depth perception doesn’t need any recognizable objects. Fill the left image with random dots, copy it to make the right image, and shift a square region of the right image sideways. Each image on its own looks like pure noise, but the pair has a well-defined disparity everywhere, so a stereo algorithm has to rely on matching alone.

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
H, W = 120, 160
left = (rng.random((H, W)) > 0.5).astype(np.float64) * 255
right = np.roll(left, -2, axis=1)                 # background disparity 2
sq = (slice(35, 85), slice(55, 105))
right[sq] = np.roll(left, -6, axis=1)[sq]         # the square has disparity 6
right[:, W - 6:] = rng.random((H, 6)) * 255      # fill the strip that rolled around

def block_match(L, R, max_d, half=4):
    h, w = L.shape
    best = np.zeros((h, w), int); best_cost = np.full((h, w), np.inf)
    box = np.ones((2 * half + 1, 2 * half + 1))
    for d in range(max_d + 1):
        shifted = np.full_like(R, np.nan); shifted[:, d:] = R[:, :w - d]   # right pixel x - d lines up with left x
        cost = cv2.filter2D(np.nan_to_num((L - shifted) ** 2, nan=1e9), -1, box)
        better = cost < best_cost
        best[better], best_cost[better] = d, cost[better]
    return best

disp = block_match(left, right, 10)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(9, 2.6))
for a, im, cmap in zip(ax, [left, right, disp], ["gray", "gray", "viridis"]):
    a.imshow(im, cmap=cmap); a.set_xticks([]); a.set_yticks([])
fig.tight_layout(); plt.show()
print("median disparity inside the square:", np.median(disp[45:75, 65:95]), "  outside:", np.median(disp[5:30, 10:150]))
median disparity inside the square: 6.0   outside: 2.0

Real stereo systems add a lot on top of this, like smoothness between neighbouring disparities, checking that the left-to-right and right-to-left matches agree, and nowadays neural networks that learn the matching cost, but the core idea of searching along a row is the same. And the real setup is never perfectly side by side, which is where the epipolar geometry of Section 4.3.1 comes in, since it says where to search when the cameras are pointing in different directions.
